New Trial Experience

Cuemath's trial funnel ran entirely through Academic Counselors (ACs), creating a queue and slowing parents down. The brief: cut AC intervention by 70% without losing conversions. The redesign distributed the work across three principles — a parent-led journey, teacher-led conversions during the trial, and WhatsApp as the primary channel for parents.

01

The Problem

Every step of the trial funnel needed an AC — and the 2–3 day journey outlasted parent interest.

A parent's interest in Cuemath had a shelf life. It peaked the moment they signed up — and faded with every wait that followed. The funnel was AC-dependent at every step: qualification, slot booking, post-trial follow-up, enrollment. Each handoff added 6–12 hours of delay, and the full journey ran 2–3 days. By the end, the spark was gone.

For ACs, every parent was one of dozens that day. They juggled, prioritized, called back when they could. Cuemath ran on thin margins; hiring more wasn't viable. The funnel wasn't failing because the team was — the process needed them everywhere.

02

Role

Funnel redesign: Cuemath's trial experience — from the sign-up form through trial booking, the trial class, and post-trial enrollment. My first time owning a full user flow tied directly to business numbers.

  • End-to-end UX/UI — sign-up form, slot booking, teacher intro flow, in-trial parent surface, post-trial enrollment, feedback flows
  • Communication design — WhatsApp as the spine, with SMS and email as fallbacks; branded post-trial collateral (pricing, plans, refund policy)
  • Constraint — cut AC intervention by 70% without losing the lead-to-enrollment rate. The work had to live on WhatsApp, where teacher-parent conversations already happened
  • Cross-functional collaboration — PM (Business Head, India) on scope and metrics, FE + BE engineers on build; tutor matching algorithm sat under a separate team, I designed around it
03

Decisions

The brief was direct: cut AC intervention by 70% while holding lead-to-enrollment conversion. If the team could automate or distribute routine touchpoints (scheduling, reminders, enrollment, preference collection), ACs could focus on exceptions like special needs and payment issues. The system would scale without growing headcount.

My first hypothesis was big: have tutors handle everything from the moment they were assigned to a parent — pre-trial messaging, the class itself, post-trial enrollment, even onboarding. Parents already trusted tutors more than ACs, so tutor-as-everything seemed to follow. Testing across 22 trial classes proved it failed: pitching mid-session stretched teachers too thin, lesson quality fell, parents sensed the artifice. The shift: tutors are educators, not the operational spine. Keep them at the trust moments; distribute everything else to system and parent.

Old: sequential AC-dependent steps with 12–24h waits between each. New: parallel work across system automation, teacher relationships, and parent self-service. ACs intervene only for exceptions.

Decision 1 — WhatsApp as the spine, with automated recovery

Most teacher-parent communication already happened on WhatsApp, not in our built-in app chat. Rather than fight that pattern, we made it the system's spine. Every critical step (verification, slot confirmation, teacher intro, reminders, post-trial enrollment) moved to WhatsApp, with SMS and email as fallbacks for parents who didn't engage there. Most Indian parents don't check email reliably, so it was the last resort, not the default.

One automation rode on top of the channel: a 5-minute auto-message for parents who started but didn't book a slot — "Do you need help choosing a time?". Small, well-timed, low-judgment, capturing intent at peak before it cooled.

WhatsApp verification confirms the parent and sets up the channel.

After the trial, teachers shared branded WhatsApp messages: pricing, class formats, refund policy. Post-trial follow-up became teacher-led — no AC required.

Pricing — monthly cost with taxes, no hidden fees.

Decision 2 — Teacher as trust anchor, before and during the trial

Instead of an AC confirmation, parents got a personal WhatsApp message from their assigned teacher 24 hours before the trial — name, credentials, sometimes a short video. Rapport began 24 hours early, not after the session ended.

During the trial itself, teachers had a structured reference script — but used it only when parents asked questions. The parent pulls; the teacher doesn't push. Teachers could reference class formats (1:1, 1:2, 1:4) naturally, never as a pitch.

Teacher side — reference script: repeat the goal, let parent talk, validate and explain.

Parent side — clean conversation. Parents never see the script.

Decision 3 — Direct enrollment immediately after the trial, with teacher present

The moment the session ended, parents saw an enrollment interface on their device — not a form. Three plan options, pricing visible, one-tap payment. Captures the decision while they're still in the moment, instead of days later. No AC call. If they had questions, the teacher was still present.

A secondary win we didn't design for: with no AC in the loop, the ad-hoc discounts (10–25%) ACs used to offer when they sensed a lead slipping disappeared too — parents paid the standard price, and revenue per enrollment held firm.

05

Lessons

The shift was distribution, not optimization

The breakthrough wasn't making teachers better at selling — it was distributing the work entirely. Teachers became the trust points (pre-trial intro, in-trial presence). The system handled scheduling, reminders, and checkout. The parent owned the decision moment. ACs handled exceptions. No single role broke under the weight of the next. The question wasn't "how do we make ACs faster" — it was "who should be doing each part of this work?"

Validate assumptions before you invest five weeks in design

I spent 5 weeks designing and building the teacher-led hypothesis before testing it. A paper prototype and one 30-minute conversation with a teacher in week 1 would have surfaced the constraint immediately — teachers are stretched too thin mid-session to also sell. I lost time executing on something that wouldn't work. For my next role: any hypothesis touching live-session behavior gets a 1-hour validation conversation in week 1, before any design.

Constraints are design tools, not limitations

The 70% AC reduction mandate forced clarity: every step in the flow had to either build trust or capture intent. If it did neither, it was friction — cut it. That forced me to stop designing features and start designing a system. I'd take this lens to any optimization brief — translate the constraint into a design question, not a feature list.
