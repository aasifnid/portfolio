New Trial Experience

Cuemath's trial funnel ran entirely through Academic Counselors (ACs), creating a queue and slowing parents down. The brief: cut AC intervention by 70% without losing conversions. The redesign distributed the work across three principles — a self-serve parent journey, teacher-led conversions during the trial, and WhatsApp as the primary channel for parents.

01

The Problem

Every step of the trial funnel needed an AC — and the 2–3 day journey outlasted parent interest.

A parent's interest in Cuemath had a shelf life. It peaked the moment they signed up — and faded with every wait that followed. The funnel was AC-dependent at every step: qualification, slot booking, post-trial follow-up, enrollment. Each handoff added 6–12 hours of delay, and the full journey ran 2–3 days. By the end, the spark was gone.

For ACs, every parent was one of dozens that day. They juggled, prioritized, called back when they could. Cuemath ran on thin margins; hiring more wasn't viable. The funnel wasn't failing because the team was — the process needed them everywhere.

02

Role

I owned design for the trial funnel end-to-end — the customer need assessment form on Cuemath's website, WhatsApp/SMS/email communication, trial slot booking, teacher positioning before and during the trial, post-trial enrollment, and the post-class feedback flows for tutors and parents. The tutor matching algorithm sat under a separate team; I designed everything around it. Payment landed at the very end, after the trial and the teacher's plan discussion. This was my first time owning an end-to-end user flow tied directly to business metrics.

I worked with a lead PM (strategy and business metrics) and a small engineering team (one frontend, multiple backend) to ship. Two data points anchored the work: 40% of qualified leads didn't book a trial within 24 hours, and 35% of trial-takers didn't enroll within 3 days — both waiting on AC callbacks. The reframe: AC dependency wasn't a performance problem; it was a design problem.

Success criteria were non-negotiable: reduce AC intervention by 70% without losing the lead-to-enrollment conversion rate. One constraint shaped everything — this had to live on WhatsApp. Most teacher-parent communication already happened there, not in our app. Rather than fight that pattern, we made it the system's spine.

03

Decisions

The brief was direct: reduce AC intervention by 70% while maintaining the lead-to-enrollment conversion rate. If the team could automate or distribute the routine touchpoints (scheduling trials, reminders, enrollment conversations, preference collection), the limited AC team could focus on complex cases — students with special needs, payment issues, retention at risk. The system would scale without scaling headcount.

The unlock started upstream. I redesigned Cuemath's customer need assessment form — turning a 5-field starter (name, grade, contact, board) into structured intent capture (child, parent, language preference, schedule, expectations). With the parent's need captured fully at sign-up, a separate team's tutor matching algorithm could route the right teacher automatically — no AC call required to qualify the lead.

Old system: sequential AC-dependent steps with 12–24 hour waits between each. New system: parallel processes across system automation, teacher relationships, and parent self-service. ACs intervene only for exceptions.

The initial hypothesis and why it failed

The initial hypothesis was big: have tutors handle everything from the moment they were assigned to a parent — pre-trial messaging, the trial class itself, post-trial enrollment, and onboarding through the kid's first paid class. Parents already trusted tutors more than ACs; tutor-as-everything seemed to follow. Testing across 22 trial classes proved it failed — pitching mid-session stretched teachers too thin, lesson quality fell, and parents sensed the artifice. The deeper insight: tutors are educators, not the operational spine. The reframe: keep tutors at the trust moments (pre-trial intro, in-trial presence), distribute everything else to system and parent.

Decision 1 — WhatsApp as the primary communication channel

Most teacher-parent communication already happened on WhatsApp, not in our built-in app chat. Rather than fight that pattern, we made it the system's spine. Every critical step — verification, slot confirmation, teacher intro, reminders, post-trial enrollment — moved to WhatsApp, with SMS and email as fallbacks for parents who didn't engage there (most Indian parents don't check email reliably, so it was the last resort, not the default).

Decision 2 — Automated 5-minute recovery for abandoned slot selection

Parents signed up and verified on WhatsApp, then didn't proceed to book a slot. Feedback surfaced why: overwhelmed, distracted, uncertain about timing. A 5-minute automated follow-up triggered: "Do you need help choosing a time?" Small, well-timed, low-judgment.

Decision 3 — Teacher introduction message 24 hours before the trial

Instead of an AC confirmation, parents got a personal WhatsApp message from their assigned teacher — name, credentials, sometimes a short video. Parents weren't signing up for a generic trial; they were meeting a specific person. Rapport began 24 hours early, not after the session ended.

Decision 4 — In-trial conversation guided by a reference script, not a hard close

Teachers have talking points but only deploy them if parents ask "What comes next?" The parent pulls; the teacher doesn't push. Teachers can reference class format trade-offs (1:1, 1:2, 1:4) naturally — never as a pitch.

Teacher side — structured reference script. "Repeat the goal," "Let parent talk," then "Validate & explain." Teachers answer authentically about class formats and pricing.

Parent side — clean, conversational experience. Parents never see the script or sales messaging. Answers feel authentic because they're contextual, not memorized.

Decision 5 — Direct enrollment immediately after the trial, with teacher present

The moment the session ended, parents saw an enrollment interface on their device — not a form. Three plan options, pricing visible, one-tap payment. No AC call. If they had questions, the teacher was still present. A secondary win we didn't design for: with no AC in the loop, the ad-hoc discounts (10–25%) ACs used to offer when they sensed a lead slipping disappeared too — parents paid the standard price, and revenue per enrollment held firm.

The project shipped and the results aligned with — and exceeded — our targets.

What changed for each role

For parents — trial booking time collapsed from 48–72 hours to under 5 minutes. They could book on their own schedule, meet their teacher before the session, and enroll immediately after — all without coordinating with a human scheduler.

For teachers — pre-trial connection via the intro message became the norm. Post-trial, teachers reported no pressure to close the sale; they answered questions if asked, then parents chose enrollment on their own.

For ACs — the 70% reduction in intervention held, with clear exception-handling workflows. The team scaled without growing headcount as trial volume increased; system and teachers absorbed the load.

05

Lessons

Validate assumptions before you invest five weeks in design

I spent 5 weeks designing and building the teacher-led hypothesis before testing it. A paper prototype and one 30-minute conversation with a teacher in week 1 would have surfaced the constraint immediately — teachers are stretched too thin mid-session to also sell. I lost time executing on something that wouldn't work. For my next role: any hypothesis touching live-session behavior gets a 1-hour validation conversation in week 1, before any design.

Constraints are design tools, not limitations

The 70% AC reduction mandate forced clarity: every step in the flow had to either build trust or capture intent. If it did neither, it was friction — cut it. That forced me to stop designing features and start designing a system. I'd take this lens to any optimization brief — translate the constraint into a design question, not a feature list.

The reframe was the work

The real breakthrough wasn't making teachers better at selling. It was asking a different question: what if teachers don't sell at all? Teachers aren't salespeople; they're trust proxies. Using them that way — showing up 24 hours early, being present during the trial, answering questions after — felt authentic and worked better than any script could.

Distribute expertise across the journey

The bottleneck is often a role asked to do too many jobs. ACs were doing scheduling, follow-up, enrollment, and exception handling — all at once, all under load. Splitting the work — system for scheduling, teacher for trust, parent for choice, AC for exceptions — let each role do what it was actually good at. No single role broke under the weight of the next.
