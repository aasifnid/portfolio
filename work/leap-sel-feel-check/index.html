Feel Check

Cuemath's tutors couldn't see how students arrived emotionally — and the first 15–20 minutes of a 50-minute class went to recovering, not learning. I designed Feel Check: a student-led check-in that helps students recognize how they're feeling, name the emotion, and choose a way to regulate — all before class begins.

01

The Problem

When a student arrived stressed, tired, or distracted, the tutor had no way of knowing. Whatever was going on would surface mid-class — and by the time the tutor caught it, half the session was gone.

Class-recording reviews and ongoing tutor feedback showed it consistently: the student's state was a constant; the tutor's response wasn't. Every tutor handled it differently. There was no standard, and no tool. Combined with a top-management focus on student readiness and parent visibility, this led to the brief.

A session delivering barely half its time to actual instruction isn't the product Cuemath promised. Weaker sessions meant weaker progress — a problem worth solving on its own.

02

Role

I owned design for Feel Check end-to-end, from spec to four-phase rollout. Of Cuemath's three SEL (social-emotional learning) tools, this was the most complex: a four-stage immersive flow across two grade bands. It was designed for LEAP — Cuemath's proprietary live-class platform — and ran alongside the live session without pausing or replacing it.

Feel Check sat at the center of a broader SEL system I designed. Alongside it ran Focus Zone and Riddle Zone — the two complementary tools — connected by a feedback system that ran across all three tools and collected tutor input after every session. I also designed the supporting materials: a Tutor Guide, and Glossaries of Emotions and Heart Options that gave tutors and students a shared language for what they were feeling.

The work began with a spec from Roots Global Educational Consultancy, drafted with our PM, curriculum team, and stakeholders. It set the clinical foundation: three established frameworks — the Wheel of Awareness, RULER, and Zones of Regulation — and the learning goal we all agreed on. Building the product — how a child would actually experience this inside a 50-minute class — was the design problem, and that work was mine. I partnered with the PM on scope, Engineering on implementation, Curriculum on content, and the Motion Designer on the regulation videos.

03

Four decisions I made inside the spec's frame

The spec set the bones. These four decisions gave Feel Check the experience.

Decision 1 — Make the wheel the interface, not a reference diagram

The spec called for a "four-quadrant wheel" but didn't define the visual structure. I explored on two tracks: shape, then treatment within the picked shape.

For shape, I tested geometric grids, modal sequences, and full-screen flows — and picked the circular form, matching the look of the Wheel of Awareness itself. Within that shape, I explored how to style it: full-screen abstract atmospheres, bold icons, and finally simple line icons. Line icons were the clearest and least distracting of the three.

When students reached the regulation step, they didn't see a separate menu of activities. They saw another wheel — shaped exactly like the one they'd just used to name their feelings. The interface stayed continuous from start to finish.

Shape — what I considered

  • Grid menu — made the wheel less visible as the framework.
  • Modal sequence (one screen at a time) — broke the feeling of narrowing in on the emotion.
  • Circular wheel (picked) — the wheel itself became the surface students tapped on.

Early ideation — bringing the Wheel of Awareness to a screen, not a diagram.

Visual style — what I considered

  • Full-screen abstract atmospheres — visually rich but heavy and slow to load.
  • Bold icons — strong presence but felt childish, and hard to vary across many emotions.
  • Line icons (picked) — clear, simple, and worked across all emotion sets. Animation was pushed to v2 because we couldn't build it in the v1 timeline.

Visual styles I tried — atmospheres, bold icons, then simple line icons (picked).

In the in-house tutor test, students moved through all four stages without asking where to click. Tutors didn't have to explain how to navigate the screen — the shape of the wheel showed them.

Insight

"The wheel wasn't a diagram next to the interface — it was the interface. Students didn't read the framework; they moved through it."

From: In-house tutor test, before external rollout.

The regulation activity menu uses the same wheel shape — the framework stays continuous.

Decision 2 — Replace clinical vocabulary with words children actually use

The spec used clinical labels — "Sparkling," "Affirmation," "Set Intention." In the in-house tutor test, students hesitated, asked what they meant, or skipped these options entirely. The flow worked; the vocabulary didn't. I rewrote the labels that caused confusion before the external rollout, and took the final wording back to Roots for clinical sign-off.

What I revised

  • "Affirmation" → "Get Ready!"
  • "Set Intention" → "Set a Goal!"
  • "Sparkling" — flagged for v2; no clean alternative landed in time.

After the rollout, tutors stopped flagging confusion around the activity names.

Insight

"Vocabulary is interaction. If kids hesitate at the label, the flow is broken."

From: In-house tutor test, before external rollout.

Decision 3 — Make Unsure a state, not a fallback

"Unsure" was offered as an option at every stage of the wheel. But the original spec said: even if a student selected Unsure at every stage, they still had to name an emotion before the system could pick a regulation activity. I pushed back on that. Being unsure across all three emotional stages is an honest, common state — adults feel it too. Asking a child to name an emotion when they truly don't know isn't a check-in — it's a forced answer. And a regulation activity based on a forced answer doesn't help — it matches the made-up answer, not how the child actually feels. The team agreed. When a student selects Unsure at all three stages, the flow skips the emotion-naming step entirely and takes them to a small set of regulation activities chosen to work without a named emotion — meant for "I don't know," not skipped.

What I considered

  • Skip button — felt like failure to the student and defeated the purpose of asking how they felt.
  • Required answer (the original spec) — students would make something up; the regulation would then match the made-up answer, not the real state.
  • Curated routing (picked) — accepts "I don't know" honestly and shows activities that work without a named emotion.

Decision 4 — Audio was structural, not decorative

The first version we tested in-house shipped without background audio. That scope call came from the Motion Designer (who owned video and transition assets), along with PMs and Engineers, because building unique tracks for all 26 regulation activities across two grade bands wasn't possible in the timeline. We assumed the visuals alone could carry the regulation experience. They didn't. The session was mandatory — students couldn't skip without finishing the regulation activity — so they had two options: engage with it and finish, or sit on the screen and stall. Most stalled. Phase 1 regulation averaged 2.9 minutes of mostly-empty screen time. After reviewing 24 in-house class recordings (14 unique students, 10 repeats), I proposed a different fix: shared ambient tracks across groups of similar activities, rather than a unique track for every one. We shipped it before the external rollout.

What changed

  • Audio added: shared ambient tracks across groups of similar regulation activities.
  • Visuals: nature and abstract — rain, breathing patterns, soft light. No characters or faces.

Across Phases 2–4, regulation time dropped from 2.9 minutes to 1.9 minutes, and the reattempt rate held at around 14%. The drop in time wasn't disengagement — it was the opposite. Students were no longer stalling. The audio pulled them in, they completed the activity, and moved on.

Insight

"When the interaction itself is the thing that helps someone, the sensory layer isn't a finishing touch — it's the work."

From: In-house build, before external rollout.

05

The spec's "fallback" options were actually primary choices.

Two options the spec treated as fallbacks turned out to be primary choices, not rare ones. "Unsure" at the Body, Heart, and Mind stages was the honest answer for 1 in 5 students at the Body stage. "Do It Your Way" in the regulation menu was consistently in the top two picks across both grade bands. This is the lesson I keep coming back to — whenever a spec calls something a "fallback" now, I pause and ask whether real users would actually end up there. If the answer is yes, it's a primary path the design needs to support, not an edge case.

The audio cut was the wrong scope trade-off.

Audio sat under the Motion Designer's scope — they owned video and transition assets, and descoped it for v1 along with PMs and Engineers because building unique tracks for all 26 regulation activities across two grade bands wasn't shippable in the timeline. The constraint was real. The binary "no audio" cut was the wrong response. Once the version without audio shipped to in-house testing, I monitored 24 class recordings (14 unique students, 10 repeats) and gathered tutor feedback. Students couldn't skip the mandatory regulation activity, so they stalled — Phase 1 averaged 2.9 minutes of mostly-empty screen time. I proposed a shippable middle path: shared ambient tracks across groups of similar activities. The team picked it up; we added it before the first live cohort. Regulation dropped to 1.9 minutes, reattempt held at ~14%. The drop wasn't disengagement — it was the sensory layer pulling students in. What I take forward: when a scope cut happens in another part of the team, the user-facing effect still lands in mine. Catching it and bringing a shippable alternative back is the discipline.

Readiness was the goal. Learning outcomes were the question I left open.

Feel Check did what it was built to do — students arrived more ready to learn. We all believed that better readiness would naturally lead to better focus during the class, smoother learning, and stronger academic progress over time. But proving that wasn't the primary goal, so nobody — including me — pushed to measure it. I should have fought for a simple proxy — anything that would have connected regulation to learning, even loosely. Whether Feel Check actually moved that longer arc is the question I think about most.
