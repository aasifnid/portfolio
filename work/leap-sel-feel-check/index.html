Feel Check

Cuemath's tutors couldn't see how students arrived emotionally — and the first 15–20 minutes of a 50-minute class went to recovering, not learning. I designed Feel Check: a student-led check-in that helps students recognize how they're feeling, name the emotion, and choose a way to regulate — all before class begins.

01 The Problem

When a student arrived stressed, tired, or distracted, the tutor had no way of knowing. Whatever was going on would surface mid-class — and by the time the tutor caught it, half the session was gone.

Class-recording reviews and ongoing tutor feedback showed it consistently: the student's state was a constant; the tutor's response wasn't. Every tutor handled it differently. There was no standard, and no tool. Combined with a top-management focus on student readiness and parent visibility, this led to the brief.

A session delivering barely half its time to actual instruction isn't the product Cuemath promised. Weaker sessions meant weaker progress — a problem worth solving on its own.

02 Role

I owned design for Feel Check end-to-end, from spec to four cohort rollouts. Of Cuemath's three SEL tools, this was the most complex: a four-stage immersive flow across two grade bands, designed for LEAP — Cuemath's proprietary live-class platform — as a non-blocking module inside active tutoring sessions.

Feel Check sat at the center of a broader SEL system I designed. Alongside it ran Calm Zone and Riddle Zone — the two complementary tools — connected by a cross-tool tutor feedback flow that captured insights after every session. To anchor it all, I also designed the supporting content layer: a Tutor Guide and the Glossaries of Emotions and Heart Options that gave tutors and students a shared language for what they were feeling.

The work began with a spec from Roots Global Educational Consultancy, drafted with our PM, curriculum team, and stakeholders. It set the clinical foundation: the Wheel of Awareness, RULER, and Zones of Regulation frameworks, with a shared learning intent. Building the product — how a child would actually experience this inside a 50-minute class — was the design problem, and that work was mine. I partnered with the PM on scope, Engineering on implementation, Curriculum on content, and the Motion Designer on the regulation videos.

03 Decisions

Four decisions I made inside the spec's frame

The spec set the bones. These four decisions gave Feel Check the experience.

Decision 01

Make the wheel the interface, not a reference diagram

The spec called for a "four-quadrant wheel" but didn't define the visual structure. I explored on two tracks: shape, then treatment within the picked shape.

For shape, I tested geometric grids, modal sequences, and full-screen flows — and picked the circular form, echoing the Wheel of Awareness. Within that form, I explored visual treatment: full-screen abstract atmospheres, bold iconography, then simple line icons. Line icons won on legibility and restraint.

Students didn't see "pick an activity" beside a wheel; they saw the same wheel they'd just moved through.

What I considered

Shape

  • Grid menu — hid the framework structure.
  • Modal sequence — lost the cumulative narrowing across stages.
  • Circular wheel (picked) — framework becomes the decision surface.

Early ideation — bringing the Wheel of Awareness to a screen, not a diagram.

Treatment within the form

  • Full-screen abstract atmospheres — visually rich but heavy, slow to load.
  • Bold iconography — strong presence but read childish; limited tonal range across emotion sets.
  • Line icons (picked) — legible, restrained, scaled cleanly. Animated treatment was scoped to v2 given v1 asset timeline.

Treatment exploration — atmospheres, bold iconography, then simple line icons (picked).

In the in-house tutor test, students moved through all four stages without asking where to click. Tutors didn't have to explain navigation — the form did.

Insight

"The framework became visible as the decision structure."

From: In-house tutor test, before external rollout.

Even the regulation activity menu used the wheel — the framework, not decoration.

Decision 02

Replace clinical vocabulary with words children actually use

The spec used clinical labels — "Sparkling," "Affirmation," "Set Intention." In the in-house tutor test, students hesitated, asked what they meant, or skipped options entirely. The flow worked; the vocabulary didn't. I revised the friction points before external rollout and took the finals back to Roots for clinical sign-off.

What I revised

  • "Affirmation" → "Get Ready!"
  • "Set Intention" → "Set a Goal!"
  • "Sparkling" — flagged for v2; no clean alternative landed in time.

Activity names no longer surfaced in tutor friction logs after rollout.

Insight

"Vocabulary is interaction. If kids hesitate at the label, the flow is broken."

From: In-house tutor test, before external rollout.

Decision 03

Make Unsure a state, not a fallback

"Unsure" was an option at each stage. The spec's intent — even when a student picked Unsure on all four — was to still have them name an emotion, then map regulation to it. I pushed back. Being unsure across all four stages is an honest, common state. Asking a child to name an emotion under that condition isn't a check-in — it's a forced answer. And regulation built on a forced answer fits the coercion, not the kid. The team agreed. The all-Unsure path skips emotion-mapping entirely and routes to a curated regulation set: appropriate for "I don't know," not skipped.

What I considered

  • Skip button — read as failure; killed the self-awareness signal.
  • Required answer (the spec's original direction) — students would invent answers; regulation would fit the invention, not the state.
  • Curated routing (picked) — owns the honest answer, routes without forcing a name.

Decision 04

Audio was structural, not decorative

The first build we tested in-house shipped without background audio. My assumption: visual content would carry the regulation moment. It didn't. Tutors observed students tapping through without settling. The experience felt like a screen, not a transition. I added ambient audio before external rollout.

What changed

  • Audio added: ambient layer over regulation activities.
  • Visuals: non-humanistic — rain, breathing patterns, ambient light. Not characters.

Engaged regulation time moved from 0.8 min → 1.9 min. Reattempt rate dropped 22% → 15%.

Insight

"For experiences where the interaction is the therapeutic tool, sensory quality is design, not polish."

Post-launch · V2 signal

What real classroom use revealed about autonomy

Post-launch, "Do It Your Way" — the autonomy option in the spec — was consistently one of the top choices across grade levels and zones. ~29% of G5–8 Green-zone selections, sitting right behind "Get Ready!" and ahead of every named alternative activity. Students weren't avoiding structure; many were choosing self-direction over a named path. For v2, I'm promoting "Do It Your Way" from one of four options to a primary entry point on the activity menu.

05 Lessons

The spec's "fallback" options were primary user states.

Two of the spec's fallback options turned out to be primary, not edge cases. "Unsure" at body/heart/mind was the honest answer for 1 in 5 students at the body stage. "Do It Your Way" in the regulation menu sat consistently in the top two across grade bands. That's the one I keep coming back to — whenever a spec labels something a "fallback" now, I pause and ask whether real users will genuinely end up there. If the answer is yes, it's a primary state, not an edge.

The audio cut was the wrong scope trade-off.

It was a wrong decision, and I agreed to it. PMs and Engineers pushed to hit the v1 timeline; audio was cut and I could have pushed back but didn't. Class recordings showed students tapping through without settling. I proposed a shared-track fix across similar regulation activities. After it shipped, engagement more than doubled. What stays with me: pushing back would have cost a week of timeline. Not pushing back cost half the regulation experience.

Readiness was the goal. Learning outcomes were the question I left open.

Feel Check met its primary objective — students arrived ready to learn. We all believed that better readiness would naturally lead to better focus in class, smoother learning, and stronger academic progress over time. But that wasn't the primary expectation, so no one — including me — pushed to instrument it. I should have fought for a rough proxy that traced regulation through to learning. Whether Feel Check actually moved that longer arc is the part I think about most.
